Drivers and impacts of the most extreme marine heatwaves events

Sci Rep 10, 19359 (2020) Published: 9 November 2020 DOI: 10.1038/s41598-020-75445-3 PMID: 33168858 · PMC7653907

Abstract

Prolonged high-temperature extreme events in the ocean, marine heatwaves, can have severe and long-lasting impacts on marine ecosystems, fisheries and associated services. This study applies a marine heatwave framework to analyse a global sea surface temperature product and identify the most extreme events, based on their intensity, duration and spatial extent. Many of these events have yet to be described in terms of their physical attributes, generation mechanisms, or ecological impacts. Our synthesis identifies commonalities between marine heatwave characteristics and seasonality, links to the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, triggering processes and impacts on ocean productivity. The most intense events preferentially occur in summer, when climatological oceanic mixed layers are shallow and winds are weak, but at a time preceding climatological maximum sea surface temperatures. Most subtropical extreme marine heatwaves were triggered by persistent atmospheric high-pressure systems and anomalously weak wind speeds, associated with increased insolation, and reduced ocean heat losses. Furthermore, the most extreme events tended to coincide with reduced chlorophyll-a concentration at low and mid-latitudes. Understanding the importance of the oceanic background state, local and remote drivers and the ocean productivity response from past events are critical steps toward improving predictions of future marine heatwaves and their impacts.

Introduction

Prolonged periods of anomalously warm ocean temperatures, marine heatwaves (MHWs), are a major threat to marine ecosystems and their functioning, that have resulted in devastating and long-lasting impacts on marine systems at scales from hundreds to thousands of kilometers. Although these extreme events are by definition rare, marine heatwaves with notable ecological impacts have been occurring more frequently in recent years. Globally, over the last century, the annual number of MHW days has increased by more than 50%, explained primarily by the long-term warming of the upper ocean, although changes in variability are important in certain regions. Moreover, future projections indicate order-of-magnitude increases in the number of MHW days by the end of the century even under scenarios of strong greenhouse gas emissions mitigation. Hence there is a need to better understand mechanisms responsible for MHW occurrence and their ecological impacts.

MHWs are a rapidly developing area of research. There is growing literature examining what constitutes a MHW, the local processes that generate and maintain them, the large-scale climate drivers that remotely trigger or modulate their likelihood, historical changes and trends in future projections, and their predictability. Quantifying MHW characteristics enables relationships to be developed between events and their impacts on marine species and ecosystems. The most advanced area of MHW research revolves around the dynamics, prediction and impacts of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). ENSO events are often associated with extreme ocean temperatures that would qualify them as MHWs, although this research is generally framed in terms of interannual variability. Considerable progress has been made into understanding the underlying dynamics of ENSO, and numerical models now operationally issue seasonal forecasts of ENSO state with considerable skill. Major ENSO events have been linked to negative ecosystem impacts including mass coral bleaching events, distributional shifts in tropical tuna and other fisheries disruptions.

In general, the proximate causes of MHWs can be classified into three categories: (i) changes in the transport of heat by the ocean, such as boundary current intensification; (ii) persistent large-scale atmospheric synoptic systems; and (iii) coupled air-sea feedback processes such as ENSO events. The objective of this study is to identify and characterize the most extreme MHWs recorded over the satellite era using a standardized methodology, in order to explore common mechanisms and processes for their occurrence. We build a comprehensive database of extreme MHWs and perform a global analysis to determine if the events previously described in the literature were in fact 'unprecedented', and identify a large number of unstudied, yet potentially high-impact, MHWs.

Results

The most intense and high-category MHW

Based on the MHW categorization scheme (see "Methods"), every ¼-degree grid cell across the globe experienced at least one MHW event exceeding strong—category 2—MHW conditions during the period from 1982 to 2017. Over 70% of the ocean area experienced severe—category 3 or higher—conditions, and 10% extreme—category 4 or higher. As event severity and category depend on both sea surface temperature (SST) and on local seasonally-varying SST variability, regions with the highest recorded category MHW and highest intensity MHW (maximum SST anomalies) do not typically align. Regions of very high severity, for example in the eastern tropical Pacific, the Ningaloo Niño region and the south-central Pacific, indicate MHWs whose intensity was well above levels of background variability.

Over most of the ocean, maximum MHW intensities were between ~2.5 and ~3.7 °C (interquartile range). However, larger maximum intensities were evident. Over 5% of the ocean, the maximum recorded daily SST anomalies (SSTA) exceeded 5 °C. Regions of large maximum SSTA were often associated with areas of (i) strong meridional or zonal mean-state SST gradients, in particular along the Kuroshio and Gulf Stream Extensions, Agulhas Retroflection and Brazil-Malvinas confluence; and (ii) regions of strong poleward advection including the East Australian Current Extension and Leeuwin Current regions.

In some regions, maximum intensity MHWs were clearly related to large-scale modes of climate variability. The eastern tropical Pacific Ocean had high maximum SSTA exceeding 5 °C, associated with strong El Niño events and the suppression of upwelling, changes in surface heat fluxes and modified ocean advection. The large maximum intensity off the Angolan/Namibian coastal region was likely related to recurring Benguela Niño events in 1984, 1995 and 2010, and the 2011 maximum intensity off Western Australia was partly related to remote influences from La Niña.

There was a notable seasonal difference with regard to the most intense and highest severity events. Globally, the occurrence of maximum SSTA events peaked in the Southern Hemisphere summer: 39% of the ocean's surface experienced its most intense MHW in austral summer (DJF), compared to 23% in austral winter (JJA). In the Northern Hemisphere, 45% (9%) of the ocean experienced its maximum recorded intensity during summer (winter). In the Southern Hemisphere, the asymmetry was even more pronounced, with 62% (7%) of the ocean area experiencing events in summer (winter). Shallower oceanic mixed layer depths that typically occur during local summer were identified as a major factor in this seasonal asymmetry—a given heat flux anomaly generates a larger surface temperature change when distributed over a shallower mixed layer, resulting in the possibility of more intense MHWs.

The highest category and most intense MHWs were strongly modulated by ENSO. During the three strongest El Niño events in the 35-year satellite record, ocean areas experiencing their highest category MHW rose dramatically. In particular, during the 2015/16 El Niño, in some months over 3 million km² of the ocean surface experienced their highest ever category events—5× higher than the average monthly area. Although moderate El Niño conditions persisted for about 19% of the record, over 34% of the ocean surface area experienced its most severe MHW during El Niño periods. During La Niña periods, extreme MHWs were not suppressed globally; La Niña events were often associated with increased ocean area experiencing its highest category event in the subtropical south Pacific, Atlantic and Indian Oceans.

Longest and maximum cumulative intensity MHW events

For 80% of the ocean area, the longest recorded MHWs were between 40 and 160 days (10th–90th percentile). Across some extended regions, maximum durations were much longer (e.g., > 250 days over ~4% of the ocean), with the longest MHWs linked to El Niño events. The unprecedented northeast Pacific MHW in 2014–16 (the Blob) was an exceptionally long event, progressing from the subtropical open ocean in 2014, to the North American coast in 2015 and then offshore again in 2016, facilitated by extra-tropical to tropical teleconnections.

In addition to strong interannual variability, there was a multi-decadal increase in extreme MHW duration over the course of the record. Sixty percent of the ocean area experienced its longest duration MHW since 2010 (i.e., over the last 20% of the satellite record). This is consistent with trends in MHWs more generally and the long-term warming of the global oceans. The longest duration MHWs corresponded closely with MHWs with the largest cumulative intensities. Cumulative intensity may be a more suitable metric to understand the impacts of MHWs on marine populations particularly sensitive to accumulated stress—for example, cumulative intensity metrics like degree heating weeks are useful indicators of coral bleaching and mortality.

Largest MHW events

The largest contiguous areas experiencing MHW conditions occurred during strong El Niño periods. In particular, during the 2015/16 El Niño, the central tropical and eastern Pacific experienced MHW conditions over an area exceeding 10% of the total ocean area for more than 200 days. The largest contiguous area experiencing MHW conditions (20% of the ocean surface) occurred in March 2016, across the Indian and Pacific Oceans—an unprecedented extent that persisted for a single day, associated with the merger of multiple co-occurring MHWs. Overall, there was an upward multi-decadal trend in the spatial extent of the largest individual MHW events, which persists even when El Niño periods are excluded.

Many of the most extreme events were associated with El Niño periods. The most extreme event in terms of spatial extent occurred during the 1997/98 El Niño in the eastern equatorial Pacific (region 62). The largest contiguous area experiencing strong or greater MHW conditions covered almost 12 million km² in November 1997. Other extreme events in the eastern tropical Pacific were associated with the 1982/83 and 2015/16 El Niño events.

The analysis highlights the evolution of the extreme MHW that constituted the Blob in the Pacific. In late 2013, a strong and persistent high-pressure system caused this event to develop in region 17. In 2014–2016, extreme MHW conditions shifted eastwards and southwards (regions 38, 43, 36, 37, 54), finally merging with the 2015/16 El Niño SST signature.

Common MHW forcing mechanisms

Persistent atmospheric high-pressure systems have been indicated as a primary driver for initiating a number of prominent MHWs. By examining the atmospheric state prior to and during the identified extreme events, we found that a large proportion of the most extreme extra-tropical MHW events were similarly associated with large, persistent, high-pressure systems. These events, which occurred in the band of prevailing westerly winds, generally formed on the equatorward flanks of high-pressure systems. The anomalous geostrophic winds oppose the prevailing winds, reducing turbulent heat fluxes from the ocean, reducing vertical mixing, and inducing an anomalous poleward Ekman flow that advects warmer water into the MHW region. Additionally, high-pressure systems are associated with subsidence and reduced cloud cover, so increased insolation may also play a role in warming the near-surface ocean.

At all latitudes, suppressed wind speeds were a common factor during the formation of a large fraction (82%) of the identified MHWs. At the same time, a majority of these events (~60%) are associated with suppressed latent and sensible heat losses, which are in turn modulated by wind speed. During the decay phase, turbulent heat fluxes were clearly important: 79% (67%) of extreme MHWs were associated with enhanced latent (sensible) heat losses after the peak of the event. Latent heat flux anomalies were typically 3× larger than sensible heat flux anomalies and almost 2× larger than insolation anomalies, suggesting that latent heat fluxes were the most prominent surface heat flux term in both the build-up and decay of many MHW events.

In contrast to higher latitude events, many tropical extreme MHWs were associated with low-pressure systems and ENSO dynamics. For example, the 2011 Ningaloo Niño (region 16) involved a low-pressure system associated with anomalous northerly winds along the west coast of Australia that weakened the normal seasonal slowdown of the Leeuwin Current, maintaining anomalously strong southward transport of warm water.

Surface chlorophyll relationship with MHWs

Using the events identified in Tables 1 and S1, a majority (72%) of the events experienced anomalously low chlorophyll-a during the extreme MHWs—a level of agreement unlikely by chance (p < 0.01 based on a binomial test). All the extreme tropical MHWs (between 20°N and 20°S) had suppressed chlorophyll-a levels. At higher latitudes the response largely changes sign, with many of the extreme MHWs associated with elevated chlorophyll-a levels.

Regions with low to medium climatological nutrient levels were frequently associated with suppressed primary production, a suppression more pronounced than previously reported for coastal MHWs. By contrast, regions with high climatological nitrate levels were typically associated with elevated chlorophyll-a at the peak of the MHW, consistent with prior work in coastal regions.

Discussion

Using a consistent framework to identify and categorize MHWs, we identified the strongest, longest and most extensive events from the satellite era between 1982 and 2017. Given the long-term warming of most of the ocean over the last century, most events are likely to be the most extreme over a much longer timeframe. By combining metrics, we provided a list of events that are both the most intense/high category and have the longest duration/highest cumulative intensity.

We found that the intensity, duration and extent of extreme MHWs were strongly enhanced during El Niño periods, both in the tropical Pacific and beyond. Interestingly, many extreme MHWs were also associated with the cold phase of ENSO, as La Niña events were associated with enhanced SST in some regions. We also demonstrated that MHW intensity has a distinct seasonality whereby maximum intensity MHWs tend to occur in the calendar summer of each hemisphere. The main factor in this asymmetry is likely concurrent shallow mixed layers in local summer that facilitate larger temperature increases for a given input of heat. Minimum mixed-layer depths and most intense MHWs typically occur in the season prior to maximum climatological SST—a mismatch that may have important implications for marine species whose ecological performance depends more on exceedance of absolute rather than relative temperature thresholds.

Consistent with a few previously described MHW events, many of the extreme subtropical MHWs identified here were associated with anomalous high-pressure systems during their build-up. Several processes are potentially associated with high-pressure systems including subsidence that gives rise to adiabatic warming, reduced cloud cover and increased insolation, and wind changes that can suppress turbulent heat losses from the ocean and vertical mixing of cold deep water to the surface. Our analysis suggests that different combinations of these mechanisms are important for different MHWs.

Globally, we showed that almost all of the most extreme MHWs were associated with suppressed wind speeds during their build-up phase. In some, but not all, cases this was also related to suppressed turbulent heat losses (in particular latent heat) from the ocean. Significantly, during the decay phase of a MHW, latent, and to a lesser degree sensible, heat losses play a prominent role in cooling about 80% of the events.

Interestingly, a large majority of extreme MHWs were associated with increased downward longwave radiation both in the build-up (>90%) and the decay phase (>82%) of the events. Part of this may relate to the fact that extreme MHWs tended to cluster during El Niño periods and recent years when global downward longwave radiation is elevated. However, the warmer SST associated with MHWs will also generate local responses in the boundary layer that increase humidity, thereby generating a positive longwave feedback—i.e., the LW change is a response rather than a driver of these large MHWs.

Understanding how MHWs affect ecosystems and the provision of ecological goods and services is critical for improving predictions of MHW impacts in a future warmer ocean. We found that the majority of extreme MHWs are associated with reduced chlorophyll-a concentrations, but the effect is dependent on latitude: strong suppression at low and mid-latitudes but more frequent enhanced productivity at high latitudes. A likely explanation is that enhanced stratification of oligotrophic tropical waters further suppresses nutrient supply to the upper ocean, limiting phytoplankton growth. By comparison, at high latitudes—where phytoplankton are more often light- than nutrient-limited—stratification reduces sinking and loss of phytoplankton out of the photic zone, thereby facilitating more persistent blooms.

As MHWs continue to grow stronger, larger, more frequent and longer, understanding the linkages between their occurrence and their ecological consequences, including primary productivity, will become increasingly important.

Materials and Methods

A MHW was defined to occur when the local SST exceeded the seasonally varying 90th percentile threshold (PC90) for at least 5 days. If SST dropped below PC90 for < 2 days and subsequently returned above the threshold, the MHW was treated as a single event. A 30-year climatological period of 1983–2012 was used as the baseline to estimate percentile thresholds and mean climatological SST. Code to calculate MHW characteristics is available at github.com/ecjoliver/MHW_Drivers.

SST data were taken from the NOAA 1/4° daily Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature v2.0, which incorporates AVHRR satellite retrievals and ship observations referenced to buoy data. A severity index was defined as:

Si,t = (SSTi,t − μi,d) / (PC90i,d − μi,d)

where μi,d is the long-term daily mean SST and PC90i,d is the 90th-percentile SST threshold, both on the dth day of the year at location i. The percentile calculation used a moving 11-day window and a 31-day smoothing filter was applied to remove high-frequency noise. MHWs are thereby categorized as moderate (1 < S ≤ 2), strong (2 < S ≤ 3), severe (3 < S ≤ 4) or extreme (S ≥ 4).

A semi-objective procedure was used to characterize the most extreme events. Grid cells where the most severe recorded MHW day occurred during the largest cumulative intensity event were identified. Regions containing large near-contiguous areas of the most severe/largest cumulative intensity MHW occurring at almost the same time were manually selected. For each region, timeseries of (i) the proportion experiencing moderate or strong category MHW, (ii) the largest contiguous area intersecting the region, (iii) the integrated intensity of that area, and (iv) the number of cells experiencing their most severe recorded MHW were calculated. Start and end dates were manually identified based on when most of these metrics were elevated.

Local atmospheric processes were examined using normalized anomalies of wind speed, zonal wind, mean sea-level pressure, downward shortwave and longwave radiation, and latent and sensible heat fluxes from the ERA-Interim dataset (shortwave from ERA5), averaged over 4-week periods before and after the event peak. Surface chlorophyll-a was taken from the gridded 8-day GlobColor merged satellite product (SeaWIFS, MERIS, MODIS sensors). Long-term mean nitrate levels were extracted from the World Ocean Atlas 2013 version 2.
